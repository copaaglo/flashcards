Modern European History, Lecture 5: The French Revolution, 1789 to 1799

In the 1780s France faced a financial crisis. The government had borrowed heavily, including to support the American Revolution, and the tax system placed most of the burden on the Third Estate, the commoners, while the clergy (First Estate) and nobility (Second Estate) paid little. Bad harvests in 1788 raised the price of bread and caused widespread hunger.

To raise money, King Louis XVI called the Estates-General in May 1789, for the first time since 1614. Each estate had one vote, so the first two estates could always outvote the Third Estate. The Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly. When it was locked out of its meeting hall, its members met on a tennis court and swore the Tennis Court Oath, promising not to separate until France had a constitution.

On 14 July 1789, crowds in Paris stormed the Bastille, a royal fortress and prison, looking for gunpowder. The date is now France's national holiday. In August the Assembly abolished feudal privileges and adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which stated that men are born free and equal in rights and that sovereignty belongs to the nation.

In 1791 the king tried to flee with his family and was caught at Varennes, which destroyed trust in him. France declared war on Austria in 1792. The monarchy was abolished in September 1792, and Louis XVI was executed in January 1793.

From 1793 to 1794, during the Reign of Terror, the Committee of Public Safety led by Maximilien Robespierre executed thousands of suspected enemies of the revolution, many by guillotine. The Terror ended when Robespierre himself was arrested and executed in July 1794.

A five-member executive called the Directory governed from 1795, but it was weak and unpopular. In November 1799 the general Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in a coup, which is usually taken as the end of the revolution.
